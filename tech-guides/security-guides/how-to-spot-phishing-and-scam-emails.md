# How to Spot Phishing and Scam Emails

Phishing is when someone sends a fake email (or text or message) to trick you into giving up a password, clicking a bad link, or sending money. It is the most common way nonprofits get hacked, and it works by creating a sense of urgency so you act before you think.

The good news: you do not need to be technical to catch most of these. You just need to slow down and check a few things.

{% hint style="warning" %}
If an email pressures you to act **right now**, that urgency is itself a warning sign. Real requests can wait the two minutes it takes to verify them.
{% endhint %}

## Quick checklist before you click or reply

Run through these whenever an email feels even a little off:

* **Check the sender's real address.** The display name can say anything. Hover over (or tap) the name to see the actual email address, and look for small misspellings like `micros0ft.com` or a public address like `yourboss.ceo@gmail.com`.
* **Hover over links before clicking.** On a computer, rest your mouse over a link to preview where it really goes. If the web address does not match the company it claims to be, do not click.
* **Be wary of attachments you did not expect.** Invoices, shipping notices, and "secure documents" you were not expecting are common traps.
* **Watch for urgency and threats.** "Your account will be closed," "pay this now," or "the boss needs this immediately" are classic pressure tactics.
* **Notice odd wording.** Generic greetings like "Dear user," awkward grammar, or a tone that does not sound like the real person can all be clues.

## Common scams aimed at nonprofits

* **The "urgent request from leadership."** A message that looks like it is from your director or board asking you to buy gift cards, change bank details, or send a wire. Attackers know nonprofits move fast to help people.
* **Fake invoice or vendor change.** An email claiming a vendor's banking info has changed, hoping you redirect a real payment to the attacker.
* **Login or password alerts.** A message that you must "verify your account" for Microsoft 365, Google, your donation platform, or your bank, with a link to a fake sign-in page.
* **Donation or grant bait.** A surprise grant or large donation that asks for sensitive details or a small "processing fee" first.

{% hint style="info" %}
A real request to move money or change payment details should always be confirmed through a second channel, like a phone call to a number you already have. Never use the contact info in the suspicious email itself.
{% endhint %}

## What to do if you are not sure

1. **Stop.** Do not click links, open attachments, or reply with any information.
2. **Verify through a known channel.** Call or message the person or vendor using contact details you already trust, not the ones in the email.
3. **Report it.** Use the "Report Phishing" button in Outlook or Gmail if you have one, and let your IT contact know.
4. **Delete it** once it has been reported.

## What to do if you already clicked

Mistakes happen, and acting quickly limits the damage. If you clicked a link, entered a password, or opened something you should not have:

* **Change your password right away** for any account you may have exposed, and turn on multi-factor authentication if it is not already on. See [Why MFA is Crucial for Nonprofits](why-mfa-is-crucial-for-nonprofits.md).
* **Tell your IT contact immediately.** The sooner they know, the sooner they can protect your accounts and data.
* **Do not feel embarrassed.** Reporting fast is exactly the right thing to do, and it helps protect everyone on your team.
